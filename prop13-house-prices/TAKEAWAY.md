# Prop 13 and California House Prices — Takeaways

The obvious story: California capped property taxes in 1978, and house prices went
to the moon. The chart says the moonshot started *first*. Prop 13 didn't cause the
boom — the boom caused Prop 13.

Source: [FHFA All-Transactions House Price Index](https://www.fhfa.gov/data/hpi/datasets),
quarterly, 1975Q1 – 2026Q1. It is the only public house price series that starts
before the June 1978 vote.

## What Prop 13 actually does

Passed June 6, 1978 with about 65% of the vote:

- Caps the general property tax at **1% of assessed value**. The statewide average
  effective rate before the vote was about **2.67%**.
- Rolls assessments back to **1975–76 market value** as the base year.
- Limits annual increases in assessed value to **2% or California CPI, whichever is
  lower** — regardless of what the house is actually worth.
- Reassessment at full market value happens **only on change of ownership or new
  construction**.
- Requires a two-thirds legislative vote for state tax increases and two-thirds
  voter approval for local special taxes.

Property tax receipts fell **more than 60%** in 1978-79, from roughly $10.3 billion
to about $5 billion.

## By the numbers

| Period | California | United States | CA relative to US |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1975Q1 → 1978Q2 (the 3.25 years *before* the vote) | **+82.5%** | +37.6% | 0.75 → 1.00 |
| 1978Q2 → 2026Q1 (the 48 years *after*) | +1,185% | +765% | 1.00 → 1.49 |

Read the last column as how far California has run ahead of the country, pinned to
the vote: 1.00 means the two have risen equally since June 1978, 1.49 means
California has risen 49% more.

California gained **+33%** on the rest of the country in the three years before
Prop 13, and **+49%** in the 48 years since. Per year, that is roughly **9% a year
before, 0.8% a year after** — an order of magnitude faster before the vote.

Milestones since: 1.49 (1990), peak **1.88** (2006), 1.33 (2012, after California
crashed harder than the country did), 1.49 today. That last column is not plotted —
it is the gap between the two lines on the chart, and it swings too much for a
single policy from 1978 to explain.

## Why the obvious story is backwards

1. **The causation runs the other way.** Assessors marked homes to a market that was
   rising 25% a year. At a 2.67% effective rate on a rapidly reassessed value, tax
   bills doubled for people whose incomes had not. That is the tax revolt. Prop 13
   is the *response* to the price boom of 1975-78, not its trigger.
2. **No break at the vote.** California's quarterly growth in 1977 (+8.5%, +7.9%)
   was faster than in the year after Prop 13 passed. There is no kink in the line
   at 1978Q2.
3. **The long-run effects are real but slow.** Three mechanisms push prices up over
   decades, not quarters:
   - **Lock-in.** Moving resets your assessment to market, so staying put is worth
     thousands a year. Wasi & White (NBER w11108) find California owner tenure rose
     **1.04 years** relative to comparison states from 1970-2000, and **2-3 years**
     in coastal cities where the subsidy is largest. Fewer listings, tighter supply.
   - **Capitalization.** A permanently lower carrying cost is worth more house. The
     tax cut gets bid into the purchase price.
   - **Fiscalization of land use.** Cities lost their property tax base and started
     favoring sales-tax-generating retail over housing, tightening supply further.
4. **The premium is not a straight line.** It nearly doubled into 2006 and then gave
   most of it back by 2012. A single policy from 1978 does not explain a curve that
   round-trips like that. Interest rates, the tech economy, CEQA, coastal
   restrictions and local growth controls all live in this line too.

## Caveat worth stating

This is a single before-and-after comparison against a national average, not a
causal design. California differs from the US in a hundred ways that changed over
48 years. The chart is strong evidence that the *timing* of the obvious story is
wrong; it is not a measurement of Prop 13's own price effect.

## The actual takeaway

Prop 13 didn't launch California housing. It arrived because housing had already
launched — and then quietly changed who pays, and who never moves.
